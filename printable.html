The PLUG Check

Plug in, blend, or unplug? Four questions to ask before the devices come out.

When laptops live on a cart instead of in backpacks, every use is a choice. Run a lesson through P-L-U-G to decide when the tech is worth it and when paper, talk, or hands-on work will teach it better.

P

Purpose

Start with the learning goal, not the tool. If you can only describe the lesson by naming an app, the goal is not clear yet.

L

Lift

Ask what the tech adds. Does it let students do something new, or only make the same task faster or neater?

U

Universal

Check that everyone can get to it: absent students, students with no device at home, students who need supports.

G

Go time

Count the setup minutes. Passing out, logging in, and putting back can take 10 minutes of a class.

One thing I will change:

Plug in on purpose

  • Tell students why the devices are out today.
  • Teach the skill, not just the app: formatting a paper, finding a saved file, using a spreadsheet.
  • Think past laptops: robots, drones, microscopes.

Blend

  • Plan on paper, publish on screen.
  • Pair each online task with a print path for students who are out.
  • Batch device time, or run laptops as one station.
  • Offer choices: poster, model, interview, slides.
Add your own:

Unplug well

  • Gallery walks, centers, and exhibits that get students moving and talking.
  • Draft by hand so thinking shows on the page.
  • Go outside, invite an expert, or read to another class.

Why these four questions

Purpose. Backward design starts with what students should understand, then picks the activity (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005). TPACK adds that good tech use depends on its fit with the content and the teaching (Mishra & Koehler, 2006).

Lift. SAMR, PICRAT, and Triple E all separate tech that replaces a task from tech that changes what students can do. In PISA data, moderate school computer use went with better results than rare use, and very frequent use with worse (OECD, 2015). For reading, a meta-analysis found a small comprehension edge for paper (Delgado et al., 2018).

Universal. Universal Design for Learning calls for more than one way in and more than one way to show learning (CAST, 2024). 17% of U.S. teens said they often or sometimes could not finish homework for lack of a reliable computer or internet (Pew Research Center, 2018).

Go time. Laptop-cart transitions can cost up to 10 minutes of a class (Mitchelson, 2026). Used daily, that is close to one class period a week, so save the cart for work that needs most of the block.
