Before the devices come out

Plug in, blend, or unplug?

When laptops live on a cart instead of in backpacks, every use is a choice. Run a lesson through four quick questions to decide when the tech is worth it.

  • P

    Purpose

    Can you name the learning goal without naming a tool?

  • L

    Lift

    What does the tech add that paper and talk can't?

  • U

    Universal

    Can every student get to it and through it?

  • G

    Go time

    Is the learning worth the minutes it takes to get going?

    Why these four questions

    Each letter comes from a problem teachers describe when one-to-one devices shrink, and each is backed by research on how technology helps learning.

    P

    Purpose

    Backward design starts with what students should understand, then picks the activity (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005). The TPACK framework makes the same point about tools: good tech use depends on its fit with the content and the teaching, never on the tool alone (Mishra & Koehler, 2006).

    L

    Lift

    SAMR (Puentedura, 2006), PICRAT (Kimmons et al., 2020), and Triple E (Kolb, 2017) all separate tech that replaces a task from tech that changes what students can do. More is not better: in OECD's analysis of PISA data, moderate school computer use went with somewhat better results than rare use, and very frequent use went with worse (OECD, 2015). For reading, a meta-analysis of 54 studies found a small comprehension edge for paper (Delgado et al., 2018).

    U

    Universal

    Universal Design for Learning asks teachers to plan more than one way in and more than one way to show learning (CAST, 2024). Home access cannot be assumed: 17% of U.S. teens said they often or sometimes could not finish homework because they lacked a reliable computer or internet connection (Anderson & Perrin, 2018). In Mitchelson's classroom survey, fewer than a third of sixth graders had a laptop at home.

    G

    Go time

    Passing out laptops, logging in, swapping dead devices, and plugging everything back in can cost up to 10 minutes of a class period (Mitchelson, 2026). At that rate, using the cart every day costs close to one full class period a week. What students do with the remaining minutes matters too: learning deepens as tasks move from passive to active to constructive to interactive (Chi & Wylie, 2014).

    A note on the evidence. The OECD and Pew findings are survey patterns, and the paper-versus-screen edge is small. PLUG is a planning habit built on that research. It has not been tested as a program on its own.
