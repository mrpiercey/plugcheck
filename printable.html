The PLUG Check

Plug in, blend, or unplug? Four questions for elementary classrooms.

In an elementary classroom, tech shares the room with blocks, books, pencils, and the carpet. Run a lesson through P-L-U-G to decide when a screen is worth it and when the lesson will work better without one.

P

Purpose

Can you name the learning goal without naming an app?

L

Leverage

What can students do with the tech that they can't do without it?

U

Universal

Can students complete the tech task with very little teacher assistance?

G

Groundwork

Is the learning worth the minutes it takes to set up?

One thing I will change:

Plug in on purpose

  • Say what the devices are for today in one sentence.
  • Model it on the big screen first.
  • Have students make something: a photo, a voice recording, a book.
  • 3–5: teach typing, saving, and finding files.

Blend

  • Hands first, then screen: build it, then photograph and explain it.
  • Make tech one station in the rotation.
  • K–2: buddies, picture directions, badge logins.
  • 3–5: plan on paper, publish on screen.
Add your own:

Unplug well

  • Use the real thing: counters, letter tiles, picture books.
  • Write and draw by hand.
  • Turn-and-talks, gallery walks, centers.
  • Go outside, or read to a buddy class.

Why these four questions

Purpose. Backward design starts with what students should understand, then picks the activity (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005). It keeps "app time" from becoming the lesson.

Leverage. Apps teach best when children are active, engaged, and working with others (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015). Hands still matter: manipulatives help math learning (Carbonneau et al., 2013), and in pre-readers, printing letters by hand engaged the brain's reading network in ways typing did not (James & Engelhardt, 2012).

Universal. Universal Design for Learning calls for more than one way in and more than one way to show learning (CAST, 2024). Read-aloud, pictures, and voice recording let children who are still learning to read and write show what they know.

Groundwork. Elementary blocks are short, so setup minutes cost more. The American Academy of Pediatrics (2016) sets no single screen-time number for school-age children and recommends balancing media with sleep, activity, and screen-free time.
