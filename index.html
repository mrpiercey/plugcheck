Plug in, blend, or unplug?

In an elementary classroom, tech shares the room with blocks, books, pencils, and the carpet. Run a lesson through four quick questions to decide when a screen is worth it.

  • P

    Purpose

    Can you name the learning goal without naming an app?

  • L

    Leverage

    What does the tech add that hands, books, and talk can't?

  • U

    Universal

    Can every student do it on their own?

  • G

    Groundwork

    Is the learning worth the minutes it takes to set up?

Check a lesson

Think of one lesson, center, or activity you are planning. It takes about a minute.

    Why these four questions

    Tech in an elementary room is its own thing: centers and rotations, logins that small hands can't manage yet, and children who are still learning to read and write. Each letter draws on research about young learners.

    P

    Purpose

    Backward design starts with what students should understand, then picks the activity (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005). In elementary rooms the risk is "app time" becoming the lesson. TPACK makes the same point: good tech use depends on its fit with the content and the teaching (Mishra & Koehler, 2006).

    L

    Leverage

    Apps teach best when children are active, engaged, working with meaningful content, and interacting with others (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015). Hands still matter. A meta-analysis of 55 studies found that concrete manipulatives help math learning (Carbonneau et al., 2013). In a brain-imaging study of pre-readers, printing letters by hand activated the brain's reading network, and typing or tracing them did not (James & Engelhardt, 2012). For children ages 1 to 8, plain digital storybooks led to lower comprehension than paper, while digital books with story-related enhancements did better (Furenes et al., 2021).

    U

    Universal

    Universal Design for Learning asks teachers to plan more than one way in and more than one way to show learning (CAST, 2024). This is where tech can shine for young children: read-aloud, pictures, and voice recording let a student who cannot yet read or write fluently show what they know. NAEYC and the Fred Rogers Center (2012) say technology for children through age 8 should be chosen intentionally, fit their development, and stay interactive, not passive.

    G

    Groundwork

    Elementary blocks are short. In a 20-minute rotation, 5 minutes of carrying devices, logging in, and finding headphones is a quarter of the time. Minutes also add up across a child's day. The American Academy of Pediatrics (2016) sets no single screen-time number for school-age children and instead recommends balancing media with sleep, physical activity, and screen-free time.

    A note on the evidence. The handwriting study was small, and the manipulatives and storybook findings are averages across many studies. PLUG is a planning habit built on that research. It has not been tested as a program on its own.
